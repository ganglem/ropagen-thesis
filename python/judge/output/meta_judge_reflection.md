# Triangulation Reflection: NLP Metrics and LLM-as-Judge Across Interaction Modes

## 1. Agreement Across Layers

The two evaluation layers converge on one consistent finding: **Chat mode produces documents with the highest surface-level lexical and semantic similarity to the expert reference**, while simultaneously receiving the highest judge scores for scenario faithfulness and hallucination control. Chat leads on ROUGE-1 (0.5106), ROUGE-2 (0.2913), ROUGE-L (0.3892), METEOR (0.3902), BERTScore F1 (0.9041), and SBERT cosine similarity (0.9895), and the judge assigns it the best hallucination_inverse mean (2.538) and highest overall score (2.615). This alignment is substantively coherent: documents that stay closer to scenario-grounded content naturally resemble the expert reference more closely, because the expert also wrote against the same scenario.

A secondary point of agreement concerns Form mode's structural reliability. The judge awards Form the highest completeness mean (4.838) and legal correctness mean (3.595), and the NLP layer corroborates this with the highest BERTScore Recall (0.9225) — indicating that Form documents cover more of the expert reference's content than the other modes. Recall-oriented coverage and structural completeness are conceptually aligned, lending mutual support to this finding.

## 2. Divergences

The most striking divergence concerns **Form mode's hallucination profile**. The judge assigns Form the worst hallucination_inverse score (1.649) and lowest scenario faithfulness (1.838), yet the NLP metrics place Form in the middle or even slightly above Ask on several measures (ROUGE-2: 0.3060 vs. 0.2568; METEOR: 0.4124 vs. 0.3650; BERTScore F1: 0.9052 vs. 0.8969). This is most plausibly explained by **single-reference bias**: the expert reference is itself a structurally complete, legally well-phrased ROPA document. When the Form mode's LLM fabricates plausible-sounding but factually incorrect content — inventing company names, retention periods, or data categories — it may still use GDPR-standard vocabulary and document structure that overlaps lexically and semantically with the expert text. NLP metrics cannot distinguish between a correctly attributed legal phrase and a hallucinated one that happens to use the same terminology. The judge, operating against the scenario description, can detect this factual divergence; the NLP metrics cannot.

Ask mode presents a related divergence: it scores lowest on most NLP metrics yet receives a higher judge overall mean (2.486) than Form (2.405), and its hallucination_inverse (2.405) substantially exceeds Form's. The Ask mode's more conversational, less template-driven outputs may diverge lexically from the expert reference while still being more factually grounded — a distinction invisible to surface-level metrics.

## 3. Implications for Research Questions

**RQ1 — LLM support and document quality.** The two layers together reveal a structure–hallucination trade-off rather than a monotonic quality gradient. Higher scaffolding (Form) guarantees structural coverage but induces severe hallucination; lower scaffolding (Chat) preserves factual grounding but risks incomplete field coverage (completeness mean: 3.179). Neither extreme reliably produces high-quality documents for novices, suggesting that scaffolding design must balance structural prompting with scenario-anchoring mechanisms.

**RQ2a — Art. 30 field coverage.** The judge's completeness scores and NLP BERTScore Recall jointly confirm that Form mode most reliably covers mandatory fields, while Chat mode is most vulnerable to omissions (completeness mean: 3.179; worst case id=63 scored 1). Structural scaffolding demonstrably improves coverage, but at the cost documented under RQ1.

**RQ2b — Confidence–quality gap.** Survey confidence means are uniformly high across modes (Ask: 3.968; Form: 3.858; Chat: 3.797), yet judge scores reveal that overall document quality is moderate at best (all mode means below 2.7 out of 5). The gap is sharpest for Form, where legal confidence (3.580) coexists with the worst hallucination_inverse score (1.649). Novices appear to conflate structural completeness with factual accuracy — a finding the NLP metrics alone would not have surfaced.

**RQ3 — BERTScore semantic similarity across modes.** BERTScore F1 differences across modes are narrow (Ask: 0.8969; Chat: 0.9041; Form: 0.9052), and SBERT cosine similarities are even more compressed (0.9862–0.9895). These small absolute differences limit the discriminative power of semantic similarity metrics for this task. Chat mode's marginal lead on BERTScore F1 and SBERT is consistent with its better judge faithfulness scores, but the magnitude is insufficient to draw strong conclusions from NLP metrics alone.

## 4. Limitations of This Triangulation

Four limitations constrain interpretation. First, **single-reference bias** means all NLP metrics are anchored to one expert document; valid alternative phrasings are penalised regardless of their legal correctness. Second, **single-judge bias** means the rubric is operationalised exclusively by Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview; agreement with human legal experts is unknown and untested. Third, **model-family bias** is partially mitigated by using a different judge family (Google Gemini) from the generator (Mistral AI), but residual stylistic preferences cannot be ruled out. Fourth, **statistical power** is limited by N=113 across three unequal groups, and the high within-mode variance — particularly in Ask (completeness SD: 1.537) and Chat (overall SD: 1.310) — means mode-level means should be interpreted cautiously. Together, these limitations mean the triangulation is suggestive rather than definitive, and replication with multiple references, multiple judges, and legal expert validation remains necessary.
