\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This thesis set out to assess how Large Language Model (LLM)
support shapes the way novice users produce GDPR Records of
Processing Activities (ROPA). The investigation paired a
within-subjects user study of $73$ participants, each working
through all three ropagen interaction modes (Form, Ask, Chat),
with a document-quality analysis built from reference-based
NLP metrics and a supplementary LLM-as-a-judge layer. The
arrangement was deliberate: a single evaluation stream could
not have reached the question the thesis was built around,
whether what novices \emph{feel} about their work tracks what
their work \emph{is}. The findings, set out in full in
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}, can be summarized as a
redistribution-of-work story. Each mode hands the writing
task to user and model in a different proportion, and each
proportion carries a different cost; the mode with the least
LLM involvement turned out to be the one novices preferred,
the mode with the most produced the documents an independent
judge rated best overall, and the mode in the middle lost on
every front. The original hypothesis, that more LLM
involvement would improve both the experience and the output,
was right about the direction the involvement would matter
but wrong about which outcome it would move.


RQ1 asked how varying degrees of LLM support shape the novice
experience during ROPA creation. The answer is that less LLM
involvement, not more, produced the better experience on this
sample. Form was the most usable, the lightest on cognitive
load, the most preferred in the ranking task, and the fastest
to complete; four independent instruments converged on the
same verdict (see Section~\ref{sec:disc-rq1}). The consequence
is that the design intuition the thesis began with, that
adding LLM presence to the interface would make the task feel
easier, does not hold for users who lack the vocabulary to
direct the model. What helped novices most was not a more
communicative partner but a visible map of what the document
required, an option set they did not have to invent, and a
clear stopping rule.
Ask, the middle mode, is the cautionary half of this answer.
A novice cannot ask informed questions about a regulation
they have never read, and the mode that depends on the user
to drive the dialogue therefore fails the user it was
designed to help. The consequence for tool design is that
giving the user a microphone is not the same as giving them
help; an architecture that presumes domain literacy must
provide a way for the user to acquire it first.


RQ2 asked how the same redistribution of work affects the
quality of the resulting documents. The answer is that each
mode fails in a different direction, and the difference is
architectural rather than incidental. Form documents fail by
inclusion: every required field is present, but some of what
fills them was never described by the user. Chat documents
fail by omission: what is on the page is grounded in the
user's scenario, but a required paragraph may be missing.
Two independent measurement methodologies, reference-based
NLP and the reference-free judge, read the same property from
different sides (see Section~\ref{sec:disc-rq2}).
The consequence is that the choice of interaction mode is
also a choice of which kind of risk a tool prefers to take
on. An incomplete document is the worse legal risk, since it
fails the Article~30 obligation directly. A
complete-but-fabricated document is the worse operational
risk, since it commits the organization to processing details
that may not match what it actually does. Neither error is
harmless, and the chapter-level evidence shows that the
better tool is neither Form nor Chat in isolation but a
content-typed assignment that hands each Article~30(1)
sub-record to the architecture its content type demands.


RQ3 asked whether what users \emph{feel} about the AI's
output tracks what the AI \emph{actually produces}. The
answer is twofold and is the cleanest illustration of why
the breadth-over-depth design was the right one. At the mode
level, the confidence ordering and the reference-based
quality ordering do not align: the most confident users and
the least confident users produce documents whose BERTScore
F1 differs only by rounding. At the same level, the
confidence ordering does match the judge's holistic verdict,
so the hypothesis the thesis began with reappears at this
slice of the data, vindicated on the dimension it was not
originally aimed at (see Section~\ref{sec:disc-rq3}).
The sharper finding sits within Chat, the only mode where
the user genuinely shapes content. There, individual user
confidence does track individual document quality, while in
Form and Ask the same relationship is absent. Calibration is
therefore not a property of the user but of where in the
interaction architecture the user is allowed to shape
content. The consequence for design is uncomfortable: in the
mode where users felt most certain that the model had
identified the correct legal basis, the judge rated the
legal basis least correct. Subjective confidence in a domain
the user cannot independently verify is not a reliable
quality signal, and tooling that surfaces or rewards it
without an independent check risks substituting the
appearance of correctness for the fact of it.


The thesis contributes a controlled novice study to a
literature that has so far largely validated user-facing
legal AI tools through illustrative use cases. The
confidence-quality reading reported under RQ3 is exactly the
kind of finding that inspection-based validation cannot
reach, because it requires a user-experience instrument and
an independent quality assessment of the same documents the
same users wrote. The pairing is the methodological
contribution: a user-experience battery (System Usability
Scale, NASA Raw Task Load Index, confidence Likert items,
mode preferences, completion time) was held alongside a
reference-based NLP evaluation and a reference-free judge
layer, and the three streams were read against each other
rather than in isolation. The judge's role is supplementary
rather than co-equal with the user study and the NLP
metrics, but it earns its place precisely on the dimensions
the reference-based metrics structurally cannot reach, and
the agreement between the two layers on the selectivity
finding is what gives that finding its weight.
The substantive contribution sits in the content-typed
assignment that follows from the chapter-level NLP results.
The thesis is not arguing for a softer synonym of
``hybrid''; it is naming the criterion. Enumerable
sub-records (where the answer space is a small fixed list,
such as legal bases under Article~6(1) GDPR) should be
handed to a checkbox interface. Scenario-specific
sub-records (where the right answer depends on what the
organization actually does, such as retention periods or
technical and organizational measures) should be handed to
conversational extraction. The same principle generalizes
beyond ROPA to any structured legal-document task whose
sub-records can be classified along the enumerable and
scenario-specific axes.
A third contribution is architectural in a different sense.
Legal Correctness was flat across modes, because the
generator is the same LLM in all three. That finding
separates the problems a UI redesign can solve from the
problems only a model-side change can solve. Assigning
sub-records to interaction architectures by content type
is an architectural fix and moves coverage and faithfulness;
raising legal-vocabulary quality is a model-side fix, and
candidates include a stronger or fine-tuned generator,
retrieval-augmented generation against the GDPR text, and an
expert sign-off layer at submission. The separation is itself a result, and
it is the cleanest one the thesis produced: knowing which
dial moves which dimension is what allows a tool builder to
spend effort where it returns yield.


The most consequential follow-on is the obvious one: build
the content-typed hybrid this thesis describes and add it as
a fourth arm to a replication of the user study. The hybrid
collapses the architectural lesson into a single design and
puts it back into the same evaluation pipeline that produced
the lesson; whether the assignment criterion survives contact
with a real implementation is the test it now needs.
A second follow-on concerns the population the findings
speak to. Participants here were chatbot-fluent students at
a German university, and the within-Chat calibration
finding is plausibly stronger in this sample than it would
be in the small-organization staff who would actually own
ROPA documentation in practice. Replication on a more
representative compliance-officer sample is what would turn
the pattern reported here into a claim about the population
ropagen is designed to serve.
A third follow-on lifts the absolute quality ceiling.
No mode in this study reached the rubric midpoint on the
judge's holistic Overall score, so interaction redesign
alone cannot close the residual gap. The architectural
flatness of Legal Correctness points to where the next
contribution should go: retrieval-augmented generation
against the GDPR text and authoritative supervisory
guidance, paired with a mandatory expert sign-off at
submission. The realistic deployment is not a single fix
but a layered architecture in which interaction design,
model capability, retrieval grounding, and expert oversight
each carry the load on the dimensions they can move.


The picture the thesis leaves behind is one of redistribution
rather than replacement. The LLM is useful as a co-author,
not as a teacher: it can take on the parts of legal
documentation the novice cannot supply, but it cannot give
the novice the domain understanding the task presumes.
Neither a novice nor an LLM alone suffices in the making of
a domain-specific document like a ROPA. The user supplies
the account of the processing scenario in their own terms;
the model supplies the vocabulary, scaffolding, and curation
the user cannot reasonably produce; and the document that
satisfies Article~30 exists only where the two meet.
