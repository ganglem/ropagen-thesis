\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

Chapters~\ref{ch:userstudy} and~\ref{ch:docquality} answered the
first two research questions through complementary evidence
streams. RQ1 asked, \emph{How do varying degrees of LLM support
influence novice users during ROPA document creation?}, and the
User Study chapter answered it by triangulating three instruments,
the \textit{System Usability Scale}, the \textit{NASA R-TLX} workload inventory, and
the \textit{Perceived AI Support} questionnaire, against a post-experiment
mode ranking and a per-mode timing analysis. The headline finding
is that more LLM support did not improve the user experience. Form
led on usability, on R-TLX workload, on completion time, on the
post-experiment ranking, and on re-use intent. The R-TLX composite
did not reach significance, but two subscales did individually:
Temporal Demand and Frustration. The user-experience cost of more
LLM involvement therefore lands specifically on perceived time
pressure and frustration, not on workload as an aggregate. Ask and
Chat earned credit only on the AI questionnaire's content-judgment
items, and on a split pattern: Ask leads on transparency (item~3)
and trust (item~4), while Chat alone leads on legal-basis
confidence (item~5). When asked which mode they would
actually use again, participants returned to the one that had done
the least LLM work on their behalf, with Form taking the plurality
of first-place votes, a statistically significant preference of
medium effect size.

RQ2 asked, \emph{How do varying degrees of LLM support influence
the quality of ROPA documents produced by novice users?}, and the
Document Quality chapter answered it with two evidence layers:
reference-based NLP metrics, both lexical (BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR)
and embedding-based (BERTScore, SBERT), and a supplementary
LLM-as-a-judge layer. The headline finding is that more LLM
support did not produce categorically better documents either.
At the document level the metrics resolved into a
coverage-versus-precision trade-off, with Form leading recall and
Chat leading the semantic and precision measures. At the
chapter level the same metrics split the six rendered ROPA
chapters into three zones, with Ask winning Chapter~1, Form
winning Chapters~2--4, and Chat winning Chapters~5--6. The judge
layer surfaced a completeness-versus-faithfulness trade-off on
top of that, with Form rated most complete but most fabricated
and Chat most faithful but least complete; on the judge's
holistic Overall metric the three modes clustered below the
rubric midpoint with no clear winner.

What neither chapter could answer in isolation is RQ3: \emph{Is
there a discrepancy between user confidence and the document quality
actually achieved?} RQ3 is a cross-stream question by construction,
because confidence sits in the user-study stream and quality sits
in the document-quality stream, and the comparison only becomes
possible once both are on the table.
This chapter takes that comparison up. It answers RQ3 by pairing
the confidence signals from the \textit{Perceived AI Support} questionnaire
with the reference-based metrics and the judge's verdicts on the
same documents, then steps back to interpret what the combined
picture means for the design of novice-facing LLM-assisted document
tooling. Furthermore, this chapter sheds light on the Ask mode's underperformance,
situates the findings in the context of prior work, and closes
with a discussion of the study's limitations and a summary of the
main takeaways.


\section{The Confidence--Correctness Gap}
\label{sec:disc-gap}

This section answers RQ3 by layering four evidence streams:
self-reported legal-basis confidence first, then the reference-based
NLP metrics on the same documents, then the LLM judge's
reference-free verdicts (legal correctness in particular), and
finally the mode-level inversion that combines them. The pattern
the four layers expose is the headline finding of the chapter.

On the self-report side, Chat users felt the most confident that
the AI had identified the correct legal basis (item~5). Ask leads
on transparency and trust (items~3 and~4) but not on legal-basis
confidence; Chat leads specifically on legal-basis confidence and
on nothing else. Overall perceived AI support is flat across modes,
so the legal-basis ordering is item-specific rather than a general
trust effect.

The reference-based NLP metrics do not follow this ordering. Form
produces the most lexically similar documents to the expert
reference on BERTScore F1, Chat the most semantically faithful on
SBERT cosine similarity, and Ask trails on both. The mode with the
lowest legal-basis confidence is strongest on the lexical measure;
the mode with the highest confidence is strongest on the semantic
measure.

When confidence and document quality are tested against each other
directly, the correlation is essentially zero. A confident user
was no more likely to produce a strong document than an
unconfident one. The only exception is Chat, where a marginal
positive trend appears within-mode, sitting on the edge of
significance; Form and Ask show flat to mildly inverted within-mode
correlations. The pooled signal is null.

The LLM judge corroborates the NLP picture on overall quality,
with BERTScore F1 correlating strongly with the judge's holistic
score, and adds one dimension the reference-based metrics cannot
reach: legal correctness. Here the inversion is sharpest. Chat,
the highest-confidence mode, receives the lowest judge rating on
legal correctness; Form, the lowest-confidence mode, receives the
highest. This is the headline finding of RQ3: confidence about
legal basis is inversely related to judged legal correctness at
the mode level.

The Form leadership on Completeness has a structural explanation
the same judge layer makes visible. The great majority of Form
documents combine the maximum Completeness score with
moderate-or-worse Hallucination, and nearly half pair maximum
Completeness with the floor Hallucination score. Form's
checkbox UI forces structural coverage of every Art.~30(1) field
regardless of scenario fit; hallucination then enters at the
prose-generation step that turns the checked boxes into legal
text. Checkbox completeness is not the same as scenario
completeness, and the two quantities decouple at the moment the
prose is generated.

Two further findings constrain the interpretation. First, no mode
produces a document the judge would call good; all three sit below
the rubric midpoint of $3$. Reaching the midpoint or higher would
have required correct citation of the Art.~6(1) legal basis, no
fabricated retention period, and scenario-grounded technical and
organizational measures, none of which any mode reliably produced.
The comparison is therefore among three routes to a moderate
document, not among deployment-ready ones. Second, participants
rated their own task success at nearly identical levels across
modes on the R-TLX Performance subscale, while the judge's
Completeness scores diverged by close to a full rubric point.
Users could not tell, from inside the tool, which mode had actually
helped them; their judgment tracks the conversational fluency and
surface coherence the LLM produces (which Chat maximizes), not the
factual accuracy they cannot independently verify. That gap between
the signal users have and the signal they need is what motivates
the validation gate proposed below.

In sum, RQ3 is answered as follows: there is no meaningful
correlation between how confident users felt and how good their
document actually was. The discrepancy between perceived and
actual quality is real, most visible at the mode level on the
legal-basis dimension, and present even in the one mode where
within-mode calibration partially holds.


\section{Ask and the Co-Author Principle}
\label{sec:disc-ask}

RQ3 has now been answered: confidence and judged legal correctness
invert at the mode level. The remaining question is whether that
inversion is an incidental feature of three particular interaction
designs or whether it reflects something architectural about how
LLM support is distributed across user and model. Ask supplies the
clearest test, because it is the mode that strips the LLM of its
populator role and asks the user to take it on instead.

Ask was the worst mode to use, and the gap between experience and
output is the most informative finding it produces. On user
experience, Ask ranked third on the \textit{SUS}, was tied with Chat at roughly
twice Form's completion time, the most frustrating on the \textit{R-TLX}, and
the most polarized in free-text comments. On document quality, it sits between
Form and Chat on the judge's holistic score inside a narrow spread,
a moderate result at considerable cost.

The experience gap is explained by what each mode actually asks
the user to do (see Section~\ref{sec:ropagen}). Form asks the user
to categorize: tick the boxes that fit the scenario, and the LLM
generates the surrounding prose. Chat asks the user to describe:
talk through the processing, and the LLM populates the document in
the background as the conversation unfolds. Ask asks the user to
query and then to write: the LLM only answers when questioned, and
the final content must be typed by the user themselves. To use Ask
well, a novice would have needed to know what to ask, recognize a
useful answer, map fragments of a general explanation to specific
Art.~30(1) fields, and write scenario-specific German legal prose,
in short, prior GDPR knowledge. Help that requires the user to
already know what they need is not help for a novice. The
underperformance of Ask therefore makes a substantive point:
novice users benefit from an LLM that co-authors or populates, not
from one that advises.

At the chapter level, no single mode dominates across all sections
of the ROPA. Form wins Chapters~2--4, the required-field middle
where boolean scaffolding helps. Chat wins Chapters~5--6 (Retention
and Deletion, Technical and Organizational Measures), the
interpretive chapters whose content must be reasoned from the
scenario. This split motivates a section-aware hybrid: route
deterministic chapters to a minimal explainer, required-field
chapters to checkbox coverage, and interpretive chapters to LLM
extraction from a conversation.

Two design moves follow from the above. The first is a hybrid mode
that combines Form's checkbox UI with Chat-style LLM extraction.
Concretely, the user would see a checkbox grid covering the
Art.~30(1) required elements alongside a free-text description box
for the processing scenario; submitting the form would trigger
Chat-style generation that takes both the ticked options and the
free-text description as input. Form delivers the strongest
perceived productivity, Chat the strongest scenario faithfulness,
and several participants proposed the combination spontaneously.
The second is a validation gate at submission time. The judge data
show that a single-pass LLM judge can already discriminate
hallucinated documents from faithful ones; a gate that surfaces
low Faithfulness scores before the user submits would provide the
one signal novice users currently lack, without replacing expert
review.

\section{In Light of Prior Work}
\label{sec:disc-related}

The findings sit inside a small but active literature, surveyed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:background} along four lenses.

On GDPR compliance automation, von Schwerin et al.\
\cite{schwerinSystematicComparisonOpen2024} argued for
human-in-the-loop necessity on the basis of model limitations.
This thesis fixes the model and varies the interaction mode
instead, reaching the same conclusion by a different route: even
Chat sits below the rubric midpoint, so the need for human review
does not reduce to a model-choice problem. Abualhaija et al.\
\cite{abualhaijaLLMassistedExtractionRegulatory2025} reported that
high BERTScore can coexist with low BLEU when meaning is captured
but wording diverges; that pattern characterizes Chat here exactly.
On legal-document drafting and evaluation more broadly, the
consistent finding is that orchestration and staging matter more
than scale or domain pretraining, and that even careful systems
fall short of what would count as a good document
\cite{nigamStructuredLegalDocument2025, liCaseGenBenchmarkMultiStage2025}.
Both hold here. Su et al.\ \cite{suJuDGEBenchmarkingJudgment2025}
further argued that lexical metrics alone cannot capture legal
reasoning; on this sample, only the judge surfaces a different
per-mode ordering from the reference-based metrics, and the
orthogonal signal comes from the rubric, not from semantic embeddings.
Prior user-facing legal AI systems were validated through
illustrative use cases rather than controlled novice studies, and
their authors flagged that absence as a limitation
\cite{suryaAIPoweredInteractiveLegal2025, vayadandeAIPoweredLegalDocumentation2024}.
This study fills that gap; the confidence--correctness decoupling
is the kind of finding inspection-based evaluations cannot produce.

Taken together, the background chapter identified an unfilled
intersection of three concerns: GDPR-specific structured output,
novice users without legal training, and explicit measurement of
the confidence--quality gap under varying LLM support. This study
is the first to occupy that intersection, with the
confidence--correctness gap, the architectural explanation for
Ask's underperformance, and the resulting design hierarchy as its
substantive contributions.


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limits}

Five constraints bound the findings, layered from study design
through the sample, tool, and model to the metrics and judge.
These limitations bound where the confidence--correctness gap
generalizes, not whether it is real on this sample; the inversion
itself sits inside the joined RQ3 sample and survives the
cross-stream triangulation reported above.

The first concerns scope. The design pairs a user study with an
NLP-metric evaluation, and each side carries enough independent
variables that no single thread reached full depth. Breadth was
deliberate: it bought the cross-stream comparison RQ3 required,
and without it the confidence--correctness inversion would not have
been visible. The cost is that any single thread opened here, the
Ask mechanism, the within-mode confidence--quality correlations,
or the chapter-level NLP patterns, could support a study in its
own right.

The second concerns the sample profile. Participants were
IT-leaning, chatbot-fluent students at a German university,
majority male, clustered tightly in their mid-twenties, and
Bachelor-level degrees dominant. The findings
therefore generalize most directly to novices who arrive at ROPA
tooling with high prior fluency in conversational LLM interfaces
and low prior exposure to legal documentation. Practitioners
outside this profile, especially older non-IT employees in small
organizations who would actually own ROPA documentation in
practice, may show a different confidence--quality calibration;
replication on a more representative compliance-officer sample is
a natural follow-on.

The third concerns the artifact. ROPAgen is a research prototype
with known bugs and design choices that are not always optimal.
The findings split along a relative-versus-absolute line: Ask's
underperformance has an architectural cause intrinsic to the
populator-versus-advisor distinction and transfers to any
implementation that keeps the LLM passive, while UI-friction
findings could shift with a polished frontend. Replication on a
production-grade implementation, with the hybrid mode proposed in
Section~\ref{sec:disc-ask} as a fourth arm, would separate
prototype-dependent friction from the architectural pattern.
The fifth concerns the model. The findings ride on a particular
2025 Mistral checkpoint used for both the interaction dialogue and
the shared final-generation step. The relative mode-rank trade-offs
and design implications should survive a model swap, because they
rest on the interaction surface rather than on the generator's raw
quality. The absolute quality figures, with all modes below the
rubric midpoint, are tied to this checkpoint and should not be
extrapolated.

The fourth concerns the reference-based metrics. Each document is
judged against a single expert-written reference, checkbox-derived
prose that slightly advantages Form on coverage metrics. The judge
layer, which uses no reference, independently agrees with the Form
coverage finding, and Chat leads on BERTScore Precision and SBERT,
so the bias is plausibly present in the coverage metrics but does
not exhaust the pattern. A multiple-reference design would dilute
single-author phrasing idiosyncrasies; the practical obstacle is
that expert annotation at this scale is expensive and ROPA documents
are not publicly catalogued.

The fifth concerns the judge. It is a single pass of a single LLM,
without inter-rater replication, legal-expert validation, or
inferential testing of the scores. An upgraded judge would use a
multi-model ensemble with expert spot-checks and reported
inter-rater agreement. The current single-pass judge remains useful
as a supplementary signal: it agrees with the reference-based
metrics on overall quality and scores legal correctness, a dimension
the reference-based metrics structurally cannot reach. It is not a
substitute for the expert review a deployed compliance tool would
require.


\section{Summary}
\label{sec:disc-summary}

The hypothesis that more LLM support would improve both user
experience and document quality reversed in both halves. Form,
not Chat, was the preferred mode on every user-experience measure,
and how confident users felt had nothing to do with how good their
document actually was. Pooled across modes, correlations between
legal-basis confidence and both reference-based metrics are
essentially zero; the only exception is Chat, where a marginal
positive trend appears within-mode. At the mode-rank level, the
confidence--correctness inversion is sharpest on the legal-basis
dimension: Chat earned the highest confidence and the lowest judged
legal correctness; Form earned the lowest confidence and the
highest.

Ask makes the underlying principle visible by stripping the LLM
of its populator role. Novice users cannot bear sole authorship
without it, and the experience cost was substantial. The
architectural explanation transfers beyond this prototype: help
that requires the user to already know what they need is not help
for a novice.

The design implication is a co-author model, not a teacher model.
Visible structure with LLM-driven population is the hybrid the
data supports; a section-aware refinement, routing each chapter
to the mode best suited to its content, is the natural extension.
More confidence in the LLM's legal judgment did not buy better
documents, and on the dimension users were most confident about,
the mode they trusted most was the mode an independent judge rated
lowest.

The Conclusion (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}) returns to RQ1, RQ2,
and RQ3 and draws the design lessons this Discussion has framed.
