\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} answered the first two research
questions. RQ1 asked: \emph{How do varying degrees of LLM support
influence novice users during ROPA document creation?} The answer
is that more LLM support did not improve the user experience. Form,
the least LLM-driven mode, was the most usable on SUS, the
lightest on R-TLX workload, about half the wall-clock time of
either LLM-driven mode, and the most often ranked first. The
LLM-driven modes earned credit only on the AI questionnaire's
content-judgment items, with Chat highest on legal-basis
confidence; when asked which mode they would actually use again,
participants returned to Form.

RQ2 asked: \emph{How do varying degrees of LLM support influence
the quality of ROPA documents produced by novice users?} The
answer is that more LLM support did not produce categorically
better documents. The reference-based NLP metrics resolved into a
coverage-versus-precision trade-off (Form leading recall, Chat
leading semantic and precision); the chapter-level metrics split
the six ROPA chapters into three zones (Ask, Form, and Chat each
leading different sections); and the LLM-as-a-judge layer surfaced
a completeness-versus-faithfulness trade-off (Form most complete
but most fabricated, Chat most faithful but least complete). On
the judge's holistic Overall metric the three modes clustered
below the rubric midpoint with no clear winner. RQ3 asks a simpler
question: did feeling confident match making a good document?
This chapter takes up that question and what to do with the
answer. It covers the confidence-correctness gap itself
(Section~\ref{sec:disc-gap}), the co-author principle Ask makes
visible and the implications that follow
(Section~\ref{sec:disc-ask}), prior work
(Section~\ref{sec:disc-related}), constraints
(Section~\ref{sec:disc-limits}), and a summary
(Section~\ref{sec:disc-summary}).


\section{The Confidence--Correctness Gap}
\label{sec:disc-gap}

The study began with a prediction and the data ran the other way.
Section~\ref{sec:thesis-structure} of the Introduction set the
expected ordering as Chat $\succ$ Form $\succ$ Ask; the
post-experiment ranking placed Form first, Chat second, and Ask
third. Form led SUS, led R-TLX on time pressure and frustration,
took about half the wall-clock time of either LLM-driven mode, and
was the only mode on which the Perceived AI Support questionnaire
returned a clear lead, on re-use intent. The mode that does the
least LLM work for the user is the mode the user wants the most.

Three things tell the RQ3 story, looked at in order: how confident
users felt, how close their document came to the expert reference,
and what an independent LLM judge made of the result. The
confidence and NLP signals are the primary pair; the judge enters
afterward as a third lens, useful because it converges with the
reference-based picture on overall quality and adds an independent
signal on legal correctness specifically.

Chat users felt the most confident that the AI had picked the
right legal basis; Form users felt the least confident; overall
trust in the AI was about the same across modes. The primary
self-report confidence measure was item 5 of the Perceived AI
Support questionnaire (``I am confident that the AI identified the
correct legal basis''), with the questionnaire's overall
\texttt{AI\_support} mean (items 1--6) as a robustness check. On
the joined document-survey subset used for the RQ3 triangulation
(n~$=102$; Form $34$, Ask $33$, Chat $35$; $44$~unique
participants), participants rated Chat highest on item 5, Ask
second, and Form lowest. The overall \texttt{AI\_support} mean was
essentially flat across modes: the confidence ordering across
modes is a property of the legal-basis item, not of perceived AI
support in general (per-mode means in Appendix~\ref{app:inf-corr}).

The NLP metrics do not follow the confidence ranking. Form's
documents come out closest to the expert reference word-for-word;
Chat's come out closest in meaning; Ask trails on both. Form leads
BERTScore F1, with Chat just behind and Ask trailing; on SBERT
cosine similarity the order shifts to Chat first, Form second, and
Ask last (per-mode metric means in Appendix~\ref{app:inf-corr}).
The mode with the lowest legal-basis confidence is best on the
lexical-semantic measure; the mode with the highest legal-basis
confidence is best on the document-level semantic measure; the
middle-confidence mode trails on both. Confidence and NLP agree
partially on Chat and disagree on Form.

How confident users felt had nothing to do with how good their
document actually was. High confidence did not predict document
quality in either direction; a confident user was just as likely
to produce a weak document as a strong one, and vice versa. Pooled
across modes, the correlations between confidence and the
reference-based metrics are essentially zero and not significant;
only Chat shows any positive within-mode trend, and even there
only marginally. Form and Ask show flat to mildly inverted
within-mode correlations; Chat's positive trend on the
legal-basis item against BERTScore F1 sits on the edge of
significance. Full correlation tables (pooled and per-mode) are
in Appendix~\ref{app:inf-corr}.

The LLM judge enters as a third opinion, not a verdict. It agrees
with the NLP metrics on overall quality, which is what earns it a
place in the triangulation, and it can score one thing the NLP
metrics cannot: whether the legal basis is correct. Pooled across
modes, BERTScore F1 and SBERT both correlate positively with the
judge's \texttt{overall} score, strongly in the case of BERTScore
F1. The judge is not contradicting the NLP measures; it tracks
them on the holistic dimension. Because the judge needs no
reference, it can score a dimension the reference-based metrics
structurally cannot: legal correctness. On the same n~$=102$
subset, the judge rates Form highest on legal correctness, Ask
close behind, and Chat lowest; on the judge's holistic
\texttt{overall} score the order flips, with Chat leading, Ask
second, and Form last. All three sit below the midpoint of the
judge's rubric (per-mode judge means and judge-NLP correlations
in Appendix~\ref{app:inf-corr}).

Inside Chat alone, users who felt more confident did tend to
produce better documents. In Form and Ask, the link is flat or
slightly the wrong way around. The famous confidence-quality
inversion lives in one specific place: confidence about legal
basis versus the judge's verdict on legal correctness. Chat is
the only mode with significant within-mode confidence-quality
alignment, on both the legal-basis item against judge legal
correctness and the \texttt{AI\_support} composite against the
judge's \texttt{overall} score. Form shows weak positive trends
and Ask weak negative ones, none significant. Where the inversion
lives, then, is at the mode-rank level on a single dimension:
Chat is highest on legal-basis confidence but lowest on judged
legal correctness; Form is lowest on legal-basis confidence but
highest on judged legal correctness. That is the headline
inversion the original framing identified, and it survives the
triangulation: it is visible only when confidence is paired with
the judge's legal-correctness rubric, not with the reference-based
NLP metrics (per-mode confidence-judge correlations in
Appendix~\ref{app:inf-corr}).

Two participants make the gap visible at document scale. One
produced the study's strongest document on every measure; the
other produced a complete-looking document filled with details the
scenario never mentioned, which the NLP pipeline did not catch.
IAR1116's Chat document is the case-study ceiling on both NLP and
judge readings: highest semantic similarity to the reference,
highest scenario faithfulness on the judge rubric, and the
participant's strongest output across the metric suite. AEB2451's
Form document is the case-study floor on the judge rubric:
maximum Completeness, lowest Faithfulness and
Hallucination-inverse, with invented financial data, biometric
data, video surveillance, and named technical standards absent
from the scenario. The Retention and Purpose passages reproduced
in Figures~\ref{fig:worked-lexical-a}--\ref{fig:worked-semantic-b}
show both participants at passage scale; the mechanism behind the
pattern is taken up in Section~\ref{sec:disc-ask}.

None of the three modes produced a document the judge would call
good. All three sit well below the rubric's good line. What
follows compares three routes to a moderate document, not three
deployment-ready ones. The judge's holistic Overall scores all
sit below the rubric midpoint and well short of the rubric's
``good'' anchor; the spread across modes is small relative to the
gap that separates every mode from a passing document.

Participants thought they had done about equally well in every
mode. The judge says they had not, by close to a full rubric
point. From inside the tool, users could not tell which mode
actually helped them. The R-TLX Performance subscale showed the
calibration mismatch inside a single instrument: participants
rated their own task success at nearly identical levels across
modes, while the judge's Completeness verdict on the same
documents diverged by about a full rubric point.

Mode by mode, the reconciled picture runs as follows. Form users
were the least confident but produced the most reference-shaped
documents and the cleanest legal basis; the catch is that the same
documents had the most invented detail, which is why the judge's
holistic verdict on them was the lowest. Ask sat in the middle on
confidence and trailed on both reference-based NLP metrics;
within-mode confidence trends were flat or mildly inverted; it is
the mode least supported by any of the three quality lenses. Chat
users felt the most confident and produced the most
scenario-faithful documents, and it was the only mode where users
who felt better actually did better, but the judge rated its
legal correctness lowest. Users felt best about their Chat work,
sometimes for good reason (within-mode calibration) and sometimes
not (mode-level inversion on legal correctness).

The three modes win at different things. Form wins on user
experience and on covering the required fields. Chat wins on
staying close to the scenario in meaning and on legal-basis
confidence. Ask wins on nothing. The two finding streams, user
preference and document quality, do not contradict each other;
they answer different questions about the same set of documents.
Form covered the Article~30 fields most thoroughly and was the
mode users wanted to keep, but it scored lowest on confidence in
the LLM's legal judgment and produced the most fabricated
documents. Chat stayed closest to the scenario in semantic and
faithfulness terms and earned the highest confidence, but it
covered the fewest fields and the judge rated its legal
correctness lowest. Ask sat between the two on document quality
and worst on user experience. The sample is more chatbot-fluent
than the small or medium-sized organization staff member the tool
ultimately targets (mostly Bachelor-level in IT or Computer
Science, with high chatbot familiarity;
Section~\ref{sec:eval-demographics}), so the Form-over-Chat
preference reported here is conservative for the broader audience
the Article~30 obligation falls on
(Section~\ref{sec:background-summary}).

Ultimately, this is the answer to RQ3: there is no correlation
between how confident users felt and how good their document
actually was. A confident user was just as likely to produce a
weak document as a strong one, and vice versa. The only mode
where the link held at all was Chat, and even there only
marginally. Feeling confident about what the LLM produced was
not a reliable signal that the document was any good.


\section{Ask and the Co-Author Principle}
\label{sec:disc-ask}

Ask was the worst mode to use. The documents that came out of it
were ordinary; the experience of producing them was not. The
copy-paste pain users described is the visible symptom, not the
cause. On user experience Ask ranked third, was the slowest on
completion time, the most frustrating on R-TLX, and the most
polarized in the free-text. On document quality the picture is
moderate; Ask sits between Form and Chat on the judge's holistic
Overall inside a narrow spread.

Each mode puts the LLM in a different role. In Form the user picks
the categories and the LLM writes the prose. In Chat the LLM does
both. In Ask the user does both, and the LLM only answers when
asked. Ask is the only mode where the LLM is passive, and despite
that availability it made the user do more work. To use Ask well,
a novice would have needed to know what to ask, recognize a useful
answer, map fragments of a general explanation to specific
Article~30 fields, and write the scenario-specific German legal
prose. That skill set has a name: prior GDPR knowledge.

Help that requires the user to already know what they need is not
help for a novice; asking is itself a skill. The substantive
contribution of Ask is therefore what its underperformance proves:
novice users of LLM-assisted document tooling benefit from an LLM
that co-authors or populates, not from one that advises.

The fabrications do not come from the interface. They come from
the shared final generation step, the part of the pipeline the
user never sees, which writes plausible prose for every field
marked filled. All three modes hand their \texttt{DocumentData}
object to the same \texttt{finalropa} prompt at temperature
$0.05$ and top-$p$ $0.05$ (Methodology
Table~\ref{tab:ropagen-llm-params}); only the populated contents
differ. Form sets the most booleans, so the generator renders the
most fields and invents the most detail. Chat sets fewer (only
what the conversation extracts), so the generator renders fewer,
invents less, and omits more. Ask sets none, so the generator
works from the free-text alone. The Form co-occurrence of maximum
Completeness with moderate-or-worse Hallucination is the trace of
this mechanism, not a property of the checkbox UI; one mechanism,
the most parsimonious given the architecture, accounts for the
pattern. Hallucination at this scale cannot be addressed by
changing the interaction surface alone, because the fabrication
happens after the interface has handed off.

At passage scale the same pattern shows up. The participant's
faithful Chat passages score lower on similarity metrics than the
other participant's invented Form passages, because the metrics
measure shape, not truth. On Retention and Purpose alike,
AEB2451's Form passages outscored IAR1116's Chat passages on every
reported metric, even though Chat's content was faithful and
Form's was fabricated. The Retention passage cited $6$ to $10$
year statutory retention against the scenario's $3$-month rule;
the Purpose passage cited legal articles (\S~257~HGB,
\S~147~AO, \S~239~Abs.~1~HGB, \S~2~Abs.~1~Nr.~1~BDSG) absent from
the scenario. BLEU illustrates the limit sharpest: IAR1116's Chat
Retention passage shares zero $4$-grams with the reference, so
BLEU-4 zeros at the $4$-gram tier independent of the brevity
penalty. The judge layer catches what reference-based metrics
structurally cannot.

Different modes win different chapters. Ask wins the factual
contact-detail chapter. Form wins the required-field middle
chapters. Chat wins the interpretive chapters at the end, where
the content has to be reasoned from the scenario rather than
checked off a list. The chapter-level NLP metrics split the six
rendered ROPA chapters into three zones, consistent across lexical
and BERT-based families: Ask wins Chapter~1 (Controller and
Contact Details), the most factually deterministic chapter; Form
wins Chapters~2--4 (the required-field middle), where boolean
scaffolding helps; Chat wins Chapters~5--6 (Retention and
Deletion, Technical and Organizational Measures), the interpretive
chapters whose content has to be inferred from scenario specifics.

The data point to a section-aware hybrid: route each chapter to
the mode that fits its content. Deterministic-recall chapters go
to a minimal explainer, required-field chapters to checkbox
coverage, and interpretive chapters to LLM extraction from a
conversation. The simpler structure-plus-conversation hybrid
below is the necessary first step; a section-aware refinement is
the natural extension.

Two concrete design moves follow. The first is a single mode that
combines Form's visible checkbox UI with Chat-style extraction
from a conversation. The second is a hallucination check at submit
time, before the user signs off on the document.

The first implication is a hybrid mode that combines Form's
visible checkbox UI with Chat-style LLM extraction from a
conversation. Several participants proposed it spontaneously, and
the data supports it: Form delivers the strongest perceived
productivity, Chat delivers the strongest scenario faithfulness. A
hybrid that retains the boolean scaffolding (so the user sees what
is covered) and adds LLM extraction (so the document object
reaching generation is populated by what the user described) would
inherit both strengths. Hallucination still needs to be addressed
separately; the hybrid does not solve it.

A second implication is a validation gate at submission time. The
mechanism identified above pins the fabrication on the shared
generation step, the layer the user never sees. A validation gate
acts on that layer directly: the judge data show that a single-pass
single-model judge can already discriminate hallucinated documents
from faithful ones. A gate that runs such a judge against the
submitted document and surfaces low Faithfulness or
Hallucination-inverse scores before submit would give novice users
the only signal they currently lack. It is not a replacement for
expert review.

\section{In Light of Prior Work}
\label{sec:disc-related}

The findings sit inside a small but active literature, surveyed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:background} along four lenses.

Prior work on GDPR compliance automation made the case for keeping
a human in the loop on the basis of model limitations. The
findings here support that case from a direction those studies
could not produce, by testing novice users directly. Von Schwerin
et al.\ \cite{schwerinSystematicComparisonOpen2024} compared
open-source backbones for ROPA category generation and argued for
human-in-the-loop necessity on the basis of model limitations.
This thesis takes the same pipeline and extends the question from
\emph{which model} to \emph{which interaction mode}, holding the
model constant. Their human-in-the-loop claim is supported here
from a direction their study could not produce: even Chat sits
below the rubric midpoint on Overall. Abualhaija et al.\
\cite{abualhaijaLLMassistedExtractionRegulatory2025} reported, in
the adjacent problem of GDPR requirement extraction, that high
BERTScore can coexist with low BLEU when meaning is captured but
wording diverges; that pattern characterizes Chat in this study
exactly.

Prior work on legal-document drafting argues that how the LLM is
orchestrated matters more than how big it is. Holding the model
constant and varying only the interaction surface produced sharp
differences here, which supports that claim. Nigam et al.\
\cite{nigamStructuredLegalDocument2025} report a
wrapper-and-iterate design for Indian private legal-document
drafting and argue that orchestration matters over scale. The
hybrid recommendation in Section~\ref{sec:disc-ask} is the
user-side counterpart. Li et al.'s CaseGen benchmark
\cite{liCaseGenBenchmarkMultiStage2025} reports that general-domain
LLMs outperform legal-specific models, all variants still score
below $6$ out of $10$, and careful staging matters as much as
legal-domain pretraining. Both resonate here: no mode produces
what the judge calls a good document.

Prior work has argued that word-overlap metrics alone cannot
capture legal reasoning. On this sample the genuinely orthogonal
signal turned out to be the judge, not BERT versus lexical:
lexical and BERT-based metrics agreed on the per-mode ordering,
and only the judge disagreed. Su et al.\
\cite{suJuDGEBenchmarkingJudgment2025} argued that lexical metrics
alone cannot capture legal reasoning and proposed pairing them
with judgment-specific dimensions. On this sample only the judge
surfaces a different ordering (Chat ahead on Faithfulness and
Hallucination-inverse, Form ahead on Completeness). The
orthogonality comes from the rubric, not from the semantic
embeddings.

Prior work on user-facing legal AI has been validated through
illustrative use cases rather than controlled novice studies, and
the authors flagged that absence themselves. This study fills the
gap. Surya et al.\ \cite{suryaAIPoweredInteractiveLegal2025} and
Vayadande et al.\ \cite{vayadandeAIPoweredLegalDocumentation2024}
validated their designs through illustrative use cases without
controlled novice studies, and both flagged that absence as a
limitation. The confidence-correctness decoupling is the kind of
empirical finding inspection-based evaluations cannot produce.

Background named an unfilled intersection of three concerns:
GDPR-specific structured output, novice users without legal
training, and explicit measurement of the confidence-quality gap
under varying LLM support. This study is the first to occupy that
intersection. The substantive contribution is the gap itself, the
architectural explanation for Ask's underperformance, and the
design hierarchy that follows.


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limits}

Five constraints bound the findings, ranging from study-design
choices to properties of the tool and model beneath it.

The study is broad rather than deep. It pairs a user study and an
NLP-metric evaluation, and each side carries enough independent
variables (mode, instrument, metric family, case study, judge)
that no single thread reached full depth.

ROPAgen is a research prototype. Known bugs and design choices
that are not always optimal, including the one identified in the
previous section as the cause of Ask's underperformance, could
all shift the measured trade-offs in a production-grade
implementation. UI friction, prompt design, and the
boolean-scaffolding-plus-final-prose split sit in that category.

The findings ride on a particular language model. The underlying
generator is a $2025$ Mistral checkpoint, used both for the
interaction dialogue and for the shared final-generation step. A
newer or larger model would likely produce different and better
documents. The relative findings about which mode helps novices
most should survive a model swap; the absolute quality figures
(all modes below the rubric midpoint) are tied to this model.

The NLP metrics judge each document against a single
expert-written reference. That reference is checkbox-derived
prose, which slightly advantages Form's surface form on coverage
metrics. The judge, which uses no reference, independently agrees
with the Form coverage finding, so the bias is plausibly present
in the coverage metrics but does not exhaust the pattern. Chat
leads BERTScore Precision and SBERT, which is the further
evidence that the bias does not account for everything.

The judge layer itself is a single pass of a single LLM. There is
no inter-rater replication, no legal-expert validation, and the
judge's scores are not subjected to inferential testing. It is
reported as a supplementary signal throughout, triangulated
against the NLP metrics and the case study, and it is not a
substitute for the expert review a deployed compliance tool would
require.


\section{Summary}
\label{sec:disc-summary}

The hypothesis reversed. Form, the least LLM-driven mode, was
preferred. How confident users felt had nothing to do with how
good their document actually was. High confidence did not predict
document quality in either direction; a confident user was just as
likely to produce a weak document as a strong one, and vice versa.
Pooled across modes, correlations between legal-basis confidence
and both BERTScore F1 and SBERT are essentially zero; within-mode,
Chat is the only mode with positive calibration, and even there
the trend is marginal. At the mode-rank level the three quality
lenses split: Form leads BERTScore F1 despite the lowest
confidence, Chat leads SBERT and earns the highest confidence, and
Ask trails on both reference-based metrics. The supplementary
reference-free judge converges with the reference-based picture on
overall quality, which is what justifies bringing it in, and adds
an additional finding on legal correctness specifically: Chat
highest on confidence, Form highest on judged legal correctness.
That inversion is a single-dimension finding sitting on top of a
broader pattern in which more confidence does not buy better
documents. Ask makes the co-author principle visible by stripping
the LLM of its populator role; novice users cannot bear sole
authorship without it. The thesis-level claim that follows is that
LLM-assisted document tooling for novices should position the LLM
as a co-author, not as a teacher. Visible structure with
LLM-driven population is the hybrid the data supports; a
section-aware refinement, routing each chapter to the mode best
suited to its content, is the natural extension. In one sentence:
more confidence in the LLM's legal judgment did not buy better
documents, and on the dimension users were most confident about,
the mode they trusted most was the mode an independent judge
rated lowest. The full pooled and per-mode correlation tables
that underpin these claims are in Appendix~\ref{app:inf-corr}.
The Conclusion (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}) returns to RQ1, RQ2,
and RQ3 and draws the design lessons this
Discussion has framed.
