\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter answers the three research questions posed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} and puts the answers into a wider
context. Each section takes one question, gives the answer the
data supports, names the evidence that supports it, and works
through what the answer implies for the design of LLM-assisted
document tools for novices. The user-study chapter
(Chapter~\ref{ch:userstudy}) carries RQ1, the document-quality
chapter (Chapter~\ref{ch:docquality}) carries RQ2, and the two
together carry RQ3.


\section{RQ1: User Experience}
\label{sec:disc-rq1}

RQ1 asked how varying degrees of LLM support influence novice
users during ROPA document creation. The answer turned out to
run counter to the thesis's initial expectation: the mode with
the \emph{least} LLM involvement, Form, was the one novices
preferred most. The user-experience side of the evidence reaches
that verdict from four directions that rarely speak with one
voice but do so here. The SUS rated Form as the most usable mode
of the three. The R-TLX picked Form out as the lightest on every
cognitive-load dimension, with mental demand, effort, and
frustration all lowest in Form. The post-session ranking task
placed Form first on more participants' lists than either Chat
or Ask. The supplementary completion-time data confirmed the
direction: Form was also the fastest mode to use. Four
independent instruments agreeing is the closest the
user-experience side ever came to a unanimous verdict, and it
points the same way every time.

The reasons Form felt best to use are visible in what the mode
actually presents to the user. Form lays out the document's
shape openly: the fields that need filling, the options that fit
each field, and a suggest button to fall back on when the user
is unsure. Decisions remain with the user, which preserves a
sense of authorship, but the user is not asked to invent the
answer space; the interface lays it out for them. The free-text
comments collected at the end of each session captured the
feeling directly, with participants describing Form as the
clearest mode and the easiest one to know when they were
``done.'' For a novice navigating a domain they do not control,
this kind of scaffolding is what they need: a visible map of
what the document requires, a fallback when stuck, and a clear
stopping rule.

The trade-off is that Form is paradoxically also the
\emph{least} transparent mode in terms of what the LLM is
\emph{doing}. The user sees the options the interface offers,
but the model's reasoning behind any specific suggestion is not
visible to them; the LLM is closer to a silent partner than a
visible collaborator. The AI-support survey items captured this
trade-off in the perceived dimensions: Chat and Ask scored higher
than Form on transparency and on perceived helpfulness of the
AI's reasoning, but neither overtook Form on the headline
preference items. Participants noticed that Chat and Ask offered
a more communicative interaction style and said so in the
free-text comments, but they still ranked Form first and reported
they would use it again. The trade-off they accepted is real,
and it is the trade-off that ROPA tooling for novices would
have to make: visible structure for the document beats visible
reasoning from the model when the user has neither the
vocabulary nor the domain knowledge to evaluate that reasoning.

The most interesting twist in the user-experience data is on
confidence. Users felt the \emph{most} confident in Chat, not in
Form, that the AI had identified the correct legal basis. The
contradiction is only apparent. In Form, the user has full
transparency of possible answers, but the decisions still rest
in their hands, and that responsibility surfaces as uncertainty:
\emph{did I tick the right box?} In Chat, the LLM steps into the
role of a collaborator. The interaction becomes conversational,
and the user can offload both responsibility and knowledge onto
the model. That feels like partnership, and partnership feels
like competence. Whether the partner is actually competent on
the dimensions the user cannot independently verify is the
question Section~\ref{sec:disc-rq3} takes up.

Ask landed worst on every user-experience measure, and that is
not a coincidence. Ask sits structurally between Form and Chat:
the user can put open questions to the LLM about any part of the
document, but the user is responsible for what is typed into
each field. On paper it looks like the best of both worlds. In
practice it became the worst of both, and the user-experience
metrics make the failure visible. The SUS placed Ask last. The
R-TLX picked Ask out as the most frustrating mode. The
completion time put Ask at roughly twice Form's, tied with Chat.
The free-text comments were the most polarised of the three
modes, with participants reporting that they did not know what
to ask the LLM in the first place. How can a novice know what
to ask about a regulation they have never read? They cannot,
and that is the explanation in plain language: a novice with
no prior GDPR knowledge has neither the vocabulary nor the
structural awareness of Art.~30(1) to formulate questions whose
answers would help them. Failing to ask the right questions yields both
a worse experience and, as Section~\ref{sec:disc-rq2} shows, a
worse document. The user is left without a safety net, without
a collaborator, and without the sense of control that Form's
checkbox scaffolding provides.

What does this mean in a wider sense? Novice users in a domain
they do not control benefit from a guided approach, and the
shape of that guidance is recoverable from the four needs that
Form half-met and Chat half-met. They need scaffolding: a
visible map of what the document requires. They need a
collaborator: an LLM that takes on some of the writing rather
than only explaining. They need a safety net: a way to get
unstuck without already knowing the right question to ask. And
they need a sense of control: the feeling that they remain the
author, even when the model is doing most of the work. The
closest current mode to this combination is Form, but Form
sacrifices LLM transparency for it. The closest to partnership
is Chat, but Chat sacrifices the visible map. Neither delivers
all four at once, and Ask delivers none of them. The same
conclusion has been reached from a different direction by von
Schwerin et al.~\cite{schwerinSystematicComparisonOpen2024}, who
argued for human-in-the-loop necessity on the basis of model
limitations. This study reaches the same place from the
interaction side: even before the model is the bottleneck, the
interaction architecture decides what kind of help a novice
can use.


\section{RQ2: Document Quality}
\label{sec:disc-rq2}

RQ2 asked how the degree of LLM support affects the quality of
ROPA documents created by novice users. The answer is more
nuanced than RQ1 and requires three angles to put together, but
one fact runs through all of them: Ask scored worst on document
quality just as it scored worst on user experience. The mode
that failed most informatively on the user-experience side also
failed on the output side, and the reasons are linked.

The first angle is the high-level reading of the reference-based
NLP metrics. Form documents recover more of what the reference
\emph{says}: they share more surface vocabulary with the
expert-written reference, with the clearest lead on BERTScore
Recall and matching though smaller leads on ROUGE-2 and METEOR.
Chat documents stay closer to what the reference \emph{means}:
they preserve passage-level meaning even when the surface
wording diverges, with the clearest lead on SBERT cosine
similarity. Ask does neither, trailing both on every metric
that distinguishes the modes. The Form-leads-token / Chat-leads-passage
split is not a contradiction between two ``semantic'' measures
but a granularity effect: BERTScore matches at token granularity
while SBERT matches at passage granularity (see
Section~\ref{ssec:score-implications}), so each rewards a
different kind of similarity. A related observation surfaces in
the same data: the embedding-based metrics preserve the
Form-Chat-Ask ordering of the lexical metrics but compress the
absolute spread, because a narrow-domain text like a ROPA
produces embeddings that cluster tightly in vector space. The
direction of difference survives, the magnitude shrinks, and
lexical metrics retain a useful role as a coarser but
higher-variance companion to the embedding view. Form's
vocabulary lead is also partly an artifact of the reference
being itself Form-derived, a bias acknowledged in
Section~\ref{sec:disc-limits}. The same
Chat profile, high passage-level similarity coexisting with
lower token-level overlap, has been reported in adjacent
legal-document NLP
work~\cite{abualhaijaLLMassistedExtractionRegulatory2025}, so
the pattern observed here is a recurring property of
LLM-generated legal text rather than a ROPA-specific artifact.

The second angle is the precision-versus-recall pattern within
BERTScore, which tells the same story as the hallucination
finding from the LLM-as-judge layer but from a different
direction. Form leads on BERTScore Recall while Chat leads on
BERTScore Precision; the judge's reference-free rubric agrees
exactly, with Form documents rated the most hallucinated and
Chat documents rated the most faithful to the study scenario.
Two independent measurement methodologies, one reference-based
on contextual token embeddings and one reference-free on a
rubric, read the same architectural property: how each mode
distributes the selection decision between user and model. The
mechanism is the same on both sides. In Form, the user is shown
a wide set of possible options and tends to over-tick on the
``more is safer'' heuristic; the model then dutifully fills
every ticked slot with prose, including content the user never
actually described. That drives high Recall on the metric side
and high hallucination on the rubric side. In Chat, the user
cedes the curation decision to the LLM, and the LLM is better
than a novice at knowing \emph{what is necessary}. That drives
higher Precision on the metric side and lower hallucination on
the rubric side. This circles back to the user-experience
picture in a satisfying way: the low confidence users reported
in Form is consistent with overcompensation through
over-inclusion, because users who are unsure whether they have
the right information tend to add more of it. Rubric-based
judging of legal documents has a methodological precedent in
JuDGE for Chinese judgment
generation~\cite{suJuDGEBenchmarkingJudgment2025}; what this
study adds is the demonstration that the rubric layer and the
reference-based layer agree on the selectivity finding, which
strengthens it considerably. The judge's own holistic Overall
score carries an additional methodological lesson: averaging
the rubric dimensions cancels the trade-off and produces a
single number that declares no winner among the modes. If a
prospective adopter of an LLM tool asked for one quality figure,
that figure would conceal exactly the architectural difference
that should drive the design choice. The sub-metrics, not the
holistic score, are what carry the architectural signal.

The third angle is the chapter-level breakdown, which sharpens
the architectural reading further. The chapter-level NLP
results, summarized across the three headline metric families,
show the same trade-off rotating through the six Article~30
sub-records along an axis the data makes visible. The Controller
and Contact chapter is filled identically across all three modes
by the ropagen system itself, so any numerical winner there
reflects system-side assembly variance rather than a
mode-architecture property; the chapter is excluded from the
architectural reading. The middle chapters whose content is
\emph{enumerable} (Purposes and Legal Bases, Categories of
Personal Data, Data Subjects and Recipients) favor Form across
both METEOR and BERTScore F1, because the answer space is a
small fixed list of options that a checkbox UI can present
cleanly. The last two chapters whose content is
\emph{scenario-specific} (Retention and Deletion, Technical and
Organizational Measures) favor Chat across the same metrics,
because the right answer depends on what the user is actually
doing, and only a conversation that captures the scenario can
provide that grounding. Form offers checkboxes for retention as
well, with a free-text justification field attached, but the
checkbox metaphor crowds out the free-text field and novices
skip it, leaving the LLM with no scenario anchor to write from.

What does this mean in practice? Form documents err by
inclusion: every required field is present, but some of what
fills them was never described by the user. Chat documents err
by omission: what is on the page is grounded, but a required
paragraph may be missing. For a real ROPA, the costs of these
two errors pull in opposite directions. An incomplete document
is the worse \emph{legal} risk: it fails the Art.~30 compliance
requirement directly and exposes the organization to fines and
supervisory orders. A complete-but-fabricated document is the
worse \emph{operational} risk: it adds administrative load by
committing the organization to processing details that may not
match what it actually does, slows the staff who later have to
work from the document, and widens the gap between the record
and the reality the record is meant to describe. Neither error
is harmless, and they call for different remedies. The choice
of mode is therefore also a choice of which kind of risk a tool
prefers to take on, and that is a decision the tool designer
should be making knowingly rather than inheriting from the
interface metaphor.

The chapter-level result also implies what a better tool would
look like. The hybrid this thesis recommends is not ``Form and
Chat side by side'' but a routing rule: each Art.~30(1)
sub-record is sent to the interaction architecture its content
type demands. Enumerable fields use a checkbox UI;
scenario-specific fields use conversational extraction;
system-known fields are pre-filled without user interaction at
all. The wider literature on structured legal-document
generation has converged on a closely related point, that
orchestration of how the generation task is staged matters at
least as much as model scale or domain
pretraining~\cite{nigamStructuredLegalDocument2025,
liCaseGenBenchmarkMultiStage2025}; the chapter-level result here
sharpens that claim by giving the routing rule, since the
routing key is not section position in a template but whether
the content space is enumerable.


\section{RQ3: Confidence and Quality}
\label{sec:disc-rq3}

RQ3 asked whether there is a discrepancy between user confidence
and the document quality actually achieved. The answer depends
on which quality measure is laid against confidence, and the
twofold reading is itself the interesting result.

Against the reference-based NLP metrics, the answer is striking.
The mode with the \emph{lowest} confidence (Form) and the mode
with the \emph{highest} confidence (Chat) produce documents
whose BERTScore F1 differs only by rounding. Ask, the mode in
the middle on the confidence question, is the outlier on the
metric. So at the mode level, the most direct comparison of
confidence and quality yields essentially no relationship: a
user who feels more confident about the AI's output is no more
likely to have produced a better document by this measure than
a user who feels less confident.

Against the LLM-as-judge layer, the story shifts. Chat scores
highest on the judge's holistic Overall metric, which matches
the user-confidence ordering: the most confident users produced
the documents the judge rated best overall. On this comparison
the original hypothesis is confirmed, and that is the second
interesting twist in the data. The thesis began by expecting
that more LLM involvement would improve the user experience; it
did not, but it did improve the judge's holistic verdict on the
documents. The hypothesis was right about the direction. It was
just pointed at the wrong outcome.

There is a sharper finding inside this overall picture, and it
is the only inferential confidence-quality result the study
produced. Within Chat, individual user confidence reliably
tracks individual document quality: more confident Chat users
produce documents the judge rates higher. Within Form and Ask
the same correlation is absent or even faintly inverted. The
contrast is interpretable architecturally. Chat is the only mode
where the user genuinely shapes the content, so individual user
competence (loosely indexed by self-reported confidence)
actually has leverage on what the model writes. Form and Ask
largely remove the user from content generation, so individual
confidence has nothing substantive to attach to. Calibration is
therefore not a property of the user but of where in the
architecture the user is allowed to shape content.

Two caveats complete the picture. The first concerns the
dimension the confidence item literally asks about, whether the
AI identified the correct legal basis. On that dimension the
judge places Chat \emph{lowest}, not highest, so the mode users
trust most on legal correctness is the mode an independent judge
rates least correct on it. The pattern is descriptive rather
than statistically significant on this sample, but the direction
is the one a critical reader should worry about. Why would
users feel most confident on the dimension where the model
performs worst? The likely mechanism is that conversational
LLMs default to affirming user
framings~\cite{sharmaTowardsUnderstandingSycophancy2023}, and a
user whose proposed reading the model echoes back will form a
higher subjective confidence in that reading, particularly when
the content concerns a domain the user has no prior expertise
to push back with. Alongside the inversion sits a separate finding
worth naming: the flatness of Legal Correctness across modes.
Changing the interaction architecture does not move the
legal-vocabulary quality much, because the final generation step
is the same LLM in all three modes. Improving Legal Correctness
is therefore a model-side problem and not a UI-side one. A
different LLM, fine-tuning on GDPR text, or retrieval-augmented
generation against the regulation itself would all be candidates;
no choice of interaction architecture in this study moved that
dimension. This is among the cleanest separation-of-concerns
results the thesis produced.

The second caveat concerns the sample. Participants in this
study were tech-savvy and chatbot-fluent, and Chat's strong
showing in the holistic-quality data plausibly reflects that
fluency. Users who know how to talk to an LLM in a conversational
way produce better Chat outputs than users who do not, and the
within-Chat calibration finding (along with the holistic-quality
lead Chat shows against the judge) is probably stronger in this
sample than it would be in a more representative
compliance-officer population. This bounds what should be
generalised from the data, and Section~\ref{sec:disc-limits}
returns to that bound in more detail.


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limits}

Several constraints bound the findings, ranging from study
design through the sample and the tool to the metrics and the
judge. These limitations bound where the confidence-quality
findings generalise, not whether they are real on this sample.

The design pairs a user study with an NLP-metric evaluation, and
each side carries enough independent variables that no single
thread reached full depth. Breadth was deliberate, since it
bought the cross-stream comparison RQ3 required, and without it
the confidence-quality reading would not have been visible. The
cost is that any single thread opened here (the Ask mechanism,
the within-mode confidence-quality correlations, or the
chapter-level NLP patterns) could support a study in its own
right.

Participants were IT-leaning, chatbot-fluent students at a
German university, majority male, clustered tightly in their
mid-twenties, and Bachelor-level degrees dominant. The findings
therefore generalise most directly to novices who arrive at
ROPA tooling with high prior fluency in conversational LLM
interfaces and low prior exposure to legal documentation.
Practitioners outside this profile, especially older non-IT
employees in small organizations who would actually own ROPA
documentation in practice, may show a different
confidence-quality calibration, and replication on a more
representative compliance-officer sample is a natural follow-on.

The tool itself is a research prototype with known bugs and
design choices that are not always optimal. The findings split
along a relative-versus-absolute line: Ask's underperformance
has an architectural cause intrinsic to the
populator-versus-advisor distinction and transfers to any
implementation that keeps the LLM passive, while UI-friction
findings could shift with a polished frontend. Replication on a
production-grade implementation, with the hybrid mode proposed
in Section~\ref{sec:disc-rq2} as a fourth arm, would separate
prototype-dependent friction from the architectural pattern.

The findings ride on a particular 2025 Mistral checkpoint used
for both the interaction dialogue and the shared final
generation step. The relative mode-rank trade-offs and design
implications should survive a model swap, because they rest on
the interaction surface rather than on the generator's raw
quality. The absolute quality figures are tied to this
checkpoint and should not be extrapolated.

The reference-based metrics share a different limitation. Each
document is judged against a single expert-written reference,
checkbox-derived prose that slightly advantages Form on
coverage metrics. The judge layer, which uses no reference,
independently agrees with the Form coverage finding, and Chat
leads on BERTScore Precision and SBERT, so the bias is plausibly
present in the coverage metrics but does not exhaust the
pattern. A multiple-reference design would dilute single-author
phrasing idiosyncrasies, but the practical obstacle is that
expert annotation at this scale is expensive and ROPA documents
are not publicly cataloged.

The judge itself is a single pass of a single LLM, without
inter-rater replication, legal-expert validation, or inferential
testing of the scores. An upgraded judge would use a multi-model
ensemble with expert spot-checks and reported inter-rater
agreement. The current single-pass judge remains useful as a
supplementary signal because it agrees with the reference-based
metrics on overall quality and scores legal correctness, a
dimension the reference-based metrics structurally cannot reach.
It is not a substitute for the expert review a deployed
compliance tool would require.

A separate question runs across all of these constraints and
shapes what future work should attempt. No mode in this study
reached the rubric midpoint on the holistic Overall score, so
the ceiling on document quality cannot be reached by interaction
redesign alone, and the natural follow-on splits into a user
side and a system side. On the user side, the cleanest
alternative is to remove the novice from content authorship
entirely, by issuing the LLM a complete description of the
processing scenario up front and letting it generate the
document without intervening dialogue; this trades the
confidence-quality gap reported here for a different gap,
between the description the organization thought it supplied
and the processing it actually performs, but it eliminates the
novice-as-curator failure that Form and Ask both exhibit. A
weaker user-side direction, training novices before they
encounter the tool, addresses the symptom rather than the cause
and is unlikely to scale to the SME population ROPA tooling is
meant to serve. On the system side, three directions are
available, each targeting a different bottleneck. Replacing the
underlying model with a stronger or GDPR-fine-tuned LLM
addresses the absolute quality ceiling that the model paragraph
above bounds. Retrieval-augmented generation against the GDPR
text and authoritative supervisory guidance addresses the Legal
Correctness flatness reported in Section~\ref{sec:disc-rq3},
which the architecture cannot move because the generator is the
same model in all three modes. A mandatory expert sign-off at
submission addresses the residual gap that neither model nor
architecture is likely to close on their own, and converts the
LLM tool from a substitute for expert review into a means of
making expert review tractable at SME scale. The hierarchy these
directions form, not a single fix but a layered architecture in
which interaction design, model capability, retrieval grounding,
and expert oversight each carry the load on the dimensions they
can move, is the realistic target the present findings point
toward.


\section{Summary}
\label{sec:disc-summary}

Pulled together, the three answers tell a more interesting
story than the original hypothesis allowed for. The thesis
began by expecting that more LLM involvement would make ROPA
documents both easier to write and better when written. Neither
half of that expectation held.

Form, the mode with the least LLM involvement, was the one
novices preferred to use. Chat, the mode with the most,
produced the documents the LLM-as-judge rated best overall.
Ask, sitting structurally between the two, lost on every front.
The headline reading is not that more LLM is worse but that
each mode redistributes the writing work between user and model
in a different way, and each redistribution carries a different
cost. Form gives the user a visible map of the document and
pays for it with LLM opacity. Chat gives the user a collaborator
and pays for it with structural coverage. Ask gives the user
neither, because a novice with no prior domain knowledge cannot
formulate the questions that would unlock the model's help in
the first place.

What this means for the design of LLM-assisted document tools
for novices is recoverable from where each mode succeeded and
failed. Novices in a domain they do not control need
scaffolding, a collaborator, a safety net, and a sense of
control. None of the three modes tested here delivers all four
at once. A tool that did would route each Article~30
sub-record through the architecture its content type demands:
enumerable fields to a checkbox interface, scenario-specific
fields to conversational extraction, system-known fields
pre-filled without user interaction at all. The co-author
principle this design rests on is not a softer synonym for
collaboration; it is a claim about which party should hold
curation, and on the evidence here the answer is the LLM where
the user lacks the expertise to curate and the user where they
have it.

The methodological contribution sits alongside the substantive
one. Prior user-facing legal-AI tools have largely been
validated through illustrative use
cases~\cite{suryaAIPoweredInteractiveLegal2025,
vayadandeAIPoweredLegalDocumentation2024}, and their authors
flagged the absence of controlled novice studies as a
limitation. This study fills that gap. The confidence-quality
pattern reported under RQ3 is in particular the kind of finding
inspection-based validation cannot produce, because it requires
both a user-experience instrument and an independent quality
assessment of the same documents the same users wrote.

The Conclusion (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}) returns to RQ1,
RQ2, and RQ3 and draws the design lessons this Discussion has
framed.
