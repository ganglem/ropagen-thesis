\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

The study began with a prediction. Section~\ref{sec:thesis-structure}
of the Introduction set out the expectation that mode preference
would track LLM involvement: Chat $\succ$ Form $\succ$ Ask, with the
freeform conversational mode the favorite of a novice user base
that had nothing to bring to the document but their description of
the scenario. The data ran the other way. The post-experiment
ranking placed Form first ($37$ of $69$ rank-1 votes), Chat second
($20$), and Ask third ($12$); the chi-square goodness-of-fit
rejected the uniform null ($\chi^2(2) = 14.17$, $p < 0.001$,
Cram\'{e}r's $V = 0.32$, medium). The reversal is not an
instrument artifact. Form leads the System Usability Scale, leads
the R-TLX with Temporal Demand and Frustration reaching
significance individually, takes about half the wall-clock time of
either LLM-driven mode ($H = 16.46$, $p < 0.001$,
$\varepsilon^2 = 0.15$, large), and is the only mode on which the
Perceived AI Support questionnaire returns a statistically
significant lead (item~6, re-use intent). The mode that does the
least for the user, in the sense of doing the least LLM work for
them, is the mode the user wants the most.

The rest of this chapter is an attempt to make sense of that
reversal. The Evaluation chapter answered RQ1 and RQ2 with the four
user-study instruments and the two document-quality streams; this
chapter takes up RQ3, the cross-stream question of whether
self-report confidence aligns with measured document quality, and
uses it as the lens through which to read the reversal. It opens
with the gap itself, turns to the architectural reading of Ask as
a separate phenomenon that the gap framing does not on its own
explain, returns to the literature to locate the contribution
this gap represents, and closes with the design implications and
the work's limitations.

\section{The Confidence--Correctness Gap}
\label{sec:disc-gap}

The differences across modes do not spread evenly across the
measurements. They concentrate on a handful of findings, with
both Form and Chat having their moments to shine. Form clears
the SUS acceptability threshold, leads BERTScore Recall over
both LLM-driven modes, is measurably faster than either, and is
the mode participants most often want to use again. Chat leads
document-level Sentence-BERT cosine similarity over both other
modes. On most other measures the three modes are statistically
separable but practically close, and the composite tests on
SUS, R-TLX, and Perceived AI Support all fail to reach
significance even where individual items or pairwise
comparisons do. The three modes therefore produce broadly
comparable documents and broadly comparable experiences. Within
that narrow band, the variation in self-reported confidence
across modes stands out: users distinguish the modes with sharp
confidence rankings even though the documents themselves are
broadly similar. That mismatch, sharp differences in user
feeling against narrow differences in the documents, is the
substance of RQ3.

Self-report confidence in the LLM's content choices does not
match the measured quality of the documents the LLM produces.
The legal-basis confidence question, the primary confidence
measure in the user-study battery, places Form lowest, Ask in
the middle, and Chat highest. Measured document quality does not
follow that ordering. Form leads on coverage-oriented metrics,
Chat leads on precision-oriented and semantic ones, and the
judge's holistic Overall metric declares no winner among the
three. The mismatch is bidirectional, and both directions are
sharper than the user-study composite or the NLP composite alone
would suggest.

The mode users feel most confident about (Chat) produces the
least complete documents. Chat is the mode where participants
rate the AI's legal judgment most highly and where they would
least like to come back. The judge agrees that Chat documents
are the most faithful to the scenario and the least
hallucinated, but reports the lowest Completeness scores of the
three modes. Confidence is highest where coverage is lowest, and
participants have no instrument in the interface that would
signal that the documents they trust are also the most
incomplete.

The mode users prefer to re-use (Form) produces the most
fabricated documents. Form is the mode participants most often
rank first and most often nominate for re-use. The judge reports
that most Form documents combine maximum Completeness with
moderate-or-worse Hallucination, and a substantial share combine
maximum Completeness with the rubric's floor Hallucination
score. Preference is highest where fabrication is highest, and
the interface that lets users feel productive is also the
interface that hides the LLM's largest failure mode from them.

The two-participant case study makes the gap concrete. IAR1116's
Chat document reaches the case-study ceiling on the judge
rubric: the document is rated as a tight, scenario-faithful,
legally sound ROPA, and the NLP metrics agree that it is the
participant's strongest output. The user-study record is
internally consistent: the participant rated the Chat session
highly on the AI-support items and reported the same in the
free-text. All three streams align. AEB2451's Form document, by
contrast, is the case-study floor on the judge rubric: maximum
Completeness, but the lowest possible scenario-faithfulness
rating and the lowest possible hallucination-inverse rating,
with the judge noting that the document invents financial data,
biometric data, video surveillance, and named technical
standards that are absent from the scenario. The same
participant's user-study record on the Form session reports the
typical Form pattern of perceived productivity and high
preference placement. The judge's verdict on this single
document is exactly the phenomenon Form produces in aggregate.
The Retention and Purpose passages reproduced in
Figures~\ref{fig:worked-lexical-a}--\ref{fig:worked-semantic-b} of
the Evaluation chapter show the two case-study participants side
by side at passage scale; the meta-reading of those passages is
taken up in Section~\ref{sec:disc-ask}.

The gap is not between good documents and great ones. The
judge's holistic Overall score sits at $2.41$ for Form, $2.49$
for Ask, and $2.62$ for Chat, all below the midpoint of the
$1$--$5$ rubric scale and all more than $1.4$ points short of
the rubric's ``good'' anchor at $4.0$. The inter-mode spread is
$0.21$ points; the gap to ``good'' is roughly seven times that.
What this study actually compares are three routes to a moderate
document; no mode in this sample produces what the judge would
call a useful real-world Article~30 record. The reading that
follows, including the tooling implications later in the
chapter, should therefore be read as a comparison among
moderate-quality routes, not as an endorsement that any one of
them is deployment-ready for novices to produce live compliance
documents. A second illustration of the calibration mismatch
sits inside the R-TLX itself: Performance is the one workload
dimension where Form does not lead, with participants rating
their own task success as essentially identical across modes
($8.71$, $8.68$, $9.39$ on the inverted $1$--$21$ scale), yet
the judge's Completeness and Faithfulness verdicts on the same
documents diverge sharply. Participants cannot tell, from inside
the interface, which mode produced the better document.

Confidence and correctness are decoupled in this novice
population. Novices are reasonable calibrators of which mode
they want to keep using; they correctly land on Form for
productivity. They are not reasonable calibrators of whether the
LLM has produced a correct document. The interface that lets
the LLM do the most work feels the most trustworthy on the
content question and produces the tightest text, but at the
cost of coverage the user cannot audit. The interface that lets
the user do the most visible work feels the most productive,
but the LLM still fabricates around visible choices the user
did not describe. The three streams disagree on which mode is
best because they measure different things, and the disagreement
itself is the substantive answer to RQ3.

\section{Ask Underperformed Structurally}
\label{sec:disc-ask}

Where Form and Chat trade leadership, Ask trails on every NLP
metric that reaches significance and sits at the middle-to-bottom
of the judge's Overall scores. The free-text comments converge
on a single explanation, the copy-paste friction between the
chat panel and the per-section text field. That explanation is
correct as a description of the user-facing symptom, but it is
not the cause. The cause is upstream, in the design pattern Ask
embodies.

Ask positions the LLM as a teacher: it explains GDPR concepts
in plain language, offers examples, and answers follow-up
questions, with a short token budget per turn. It does not
extract structured data from the user's descriptions, does not
draft section text, and does not emit any structured update for
the front-end to merge into the document object; the
implementation is verified against the ropagen source code and
the relevant prompts are reproduced in
Appendix~\ref{app:ropagen-prompts-ask}. The user is left to
read a general conceptual explanation, map that explanation
onto their own scenario, and write the section's free-text
field themselves. For a participant with prior GDPR knowledge,
that loop would work. For a novice, the population this study
recruited, it does not. Conceptual explanation is not enough
scaffolding to produce scenario-specific legal prose. The
copy-paste friction is the visible trace of this gap:
participants were copying general explanatory text and trying
to adapt it, which is harder than starting from scratch
because the LLM never produced scenario-specific text in the
first place.

The mechanism by which the design pattern's failure becomes
visible at the metrics level runs through what each mode puts
into the document object the shared final-generation step
receives. Of the three modes, Ask is the only one in which the
predefined boolean scaffolding is never populated: the user
fills only the per-section free-text field, and the LLM
populates nothing. The document object that reaches generation
in Ask is therefore the most sparsely populated of the three,
and what gets generated reflects that. This is the consequence
of the design choice, not its cause. The cause is the choice to
use the LLM as an explainer rather than as a co-author. The
substantive claim is therefore that Ask is a structural failure,
not a technical one. The novice user cannot bear the authorial
load that this design places on them, and the documents reflect
that across every metric family.

The same argument extends to the other two modes once the
shared generation step is taken seriously. All three modes hand
their \texttt{DocumentData} object to the same
\texttt{finalropa} prompt at temperature $0.05$ and top-$p$
$0.05$ (Methodology Table~\ref{tab:ropagen-llm-params}). The
prompt is identical across modes; only the populated contents
of \texttt{DocumentData} differ. The generator is asked to
render plausible-sounding prose for every boolean it finds set,
and it does. Form sets the most booleans, because the user
clicks every applicable checkbox; the generator is therefore
asked to render the most fields, and invents the most detail to
flesh them out. Chat sets fewer booleans, because only what was
conversationally extracted reaches the schema; the generator
renders fewer fields and invents less, though it also leaves
more out (the Completeness penalty). Ask sets none, and the
generator works from the user's free-text alone. The Form
co-occurrence of maximum Completeness with moderate or worse
Hallucination ($83.8\,\%$ of Form documents) and floor
Hallucination ($17$ of $37$) is therefore not a property of the
checkbox interaction style; it is an artifact of the shared
generation step being handed maximum scaffolding to render.
The implication is consequential for the design implications
that follow. Hallucination at this scale cannot be addressed by
changing the interaction surface alone, because the fabrication
happens after the interface has handed off. Any mitigation has
to act on the layer where the invention occurs.

The two worked examples in the Evaluation chapter
(Figures~\ref{fig:worked-lexical-a}--\ref{fig:worked-semantic-b})
make this mechanism visible at passage scale. On the Retention
chapter and on the Purpose chapter alike, AEB2451's Form passages
outscore IAR1116's Chat passages on every reported metric, even
though IAR1116's content is faithful to the scenario and
AEB2451's content is fabricated: the Retention passage cites
$6$ to $10$ year statutory retention against the scenario's
$3$-month rule, and the Purpose passage cites legal articles
(\S~257~HGB, \S~147~AO, \S~239~Abs.~1~HGB, \S~2~Abs.~1~Nr.~1~BDSG)
none of which appear in the scenario. The faithful passage scores
lower because IAR1116 paraphrases (\emph{nach Austritt der
Mitarbeitenden}), while the fabricated passage scores higher
because AEB2451 draws on the same legal vocabulary as the
reference (\emph{nach Beendigung des Beschäftigungsverhältnisses,
gemäß § 257 HGB}) and then extends it with invented citations.
Form's generation path, asked to render every set boolean in
formal compliance prose, reaches for the reference's terminology
even when the content behind it is invented.

Reference-based NLP metrics, lexical and embedding-based alike,
measure surface and semantic similarity to a target document;
they are structurally blind to whether the candidate's content is
true. A document that hallucinates in the reference's vocabulary
can outscore a faithful document that paraphrases. The judge
layer is therefore not a supplementary nicety, it is the
methodological complement that catches what reference-based
metrics structurally cannot see, and it is the reason the case
study could read AEB2451's Form document as the case-study floor
on Scenario Faithfulness and Hallucination-inverse even where the
NLP pipeline does not flag it. This is the same logic motivating
the validation gate sketched in
Section~\ref{sec:disc-implications}: a gate that runs a judge
against the submitted document is acting on exactly the failure
mode the reference-based metrics cannot surface on their own.

The case study's document-level claim, that the NLP metrics agree
IAR1116's Chat is the participant's strongest output, still holds
when averaged across all six chapters; per-chapter the metrics
can flip, as
Figures~\ref{fig:worked-lexical-a}--\ref{fig:worked-semantic-b}
make concrete. The worked example
does not overturn the case-study claim, it sharpens the
triangulation argument the chapter has been making at every other
level. Reading the NLP metrics, the judge layer, and the
user-study record as three streams of partial evidence rather
than as a single verdict is precisely what makes the
confidence--correctness gap legible at all.

\section{Meta-finding and Implications for ROPA Tooling}
\label{sec:disc-implications}

Across the three modes a single design hierarchy organizes the
findings. The LLM helps when it does work; it does not help when
it tells the user to do the work themselves. When the LLM
populates structure (Chat), it produces the most faithful
individual documents. When the LLM acts as final-prose
synthesizer over user-clicked structure (Form), it produces the
most preferred user experience but the most fabricated
documents. When the LLM is restricted to explainer-only (Ask),
the novice user is left to author the document alone, and the
documents reflect that. For novice document-creation users, the
LLM has to co-author, not teach.

Three concrete tooling implications follow.

\textbf{A hybrid mode is supported by the data, not only by user
preference.} Several participants spontaneously suggested a
hybrid that combines Form's visible checkbox UI with Chat-style
LLM extraction from a conversation. The data supports the
suggestion beyond preference: Form delivers the strongest
perceived productivity, and Chat delivers the strongest
scenario faithfulness in the documents. A hybrid that retains
the boolean scaffolding (so the user sees what is covered) and
adds the LLM extraction step (so the document object reaching
generation is populated by what the user described in
conversation, not left to the model to write around bare
category labels) would in principle inherit both strengths.
Hallucination, which appears across all three modes in the
judge data, would still need to be addressed separately; the
checkbox-and-conversation hybrid does not solve it on its own.

\textbf{A validation gate at submission time.} The mechanism
identified at the close of Section~\ref{sec:disc-ask} pins the
fabrication on the shared generation step, which is the layer
the user never sees. A validation gate at submission time can
act on that layer directly: the LLM-as-a-judge data show that a
single-pass single-model judge can already discriminate
hallucinated documents from faithful ones, and a gate that runs
such a judge against the submitted document and surfaces low
Scenario Faithfulness or low Hallucination-inverse scores before
the user clicks submit would give novice users the only signal
they currently lack: whether the LLM has produced content that
matches the scenario they described. The gate is not a
replacement for expert review, but it is a plausible inline
check on the most consequential failure mode, and it acts at
the right layer.

\textbf{An education layer for novice calibration.} Self-report
confidence in this study is not well-calibrated to document
quality. An education layer that shows novice users which
fields of the final document came from their input and which
came from the LLM's generation step, with simple flags on
fields the user did not explicitly describe, would surface the
calibration mismatch in the interface rather than leave it for
the user to detect.

\section{In Light of Prior Work}
\label{sec:disc-related}

The findings reported above sit inside a small but active
literature. Reading them against the four lenses surveyed in
the Related Work section of Chapter~\ref{ch:background}
clarifies what is new here and what merely confirms what the
prior work has been converging on. The walk through the lenses follows the same
order: GDPR compliance automation, structured legal-document
generation, evaluation methodology, and user-facing legal AI.

\textbf{GDPR compliance automation.} von Schwerin et al.\
\cite{schwerinSystematicComparisonOpen2024} compared open-source
backbones for ROPA category generation and argued for
human-in-the-loop necessity on the basis of remaining model
limitations. This thesis takes the same pipeline they validated
and extends the question from \emph{which model} to \emph{which
interaction mode}, holding the model constant. Their
human-in-the-loop claim is empirically supported here from a
direction their study could not produce: even Chat, the mode
that leads the judge's Scenario Faithfulness, sits below the
rubric midpoint on Overall quality. No interaction mode
evaluated produces what the judge would call a good document
for novice authors. Abualhaija et al.\
\cite{abualhaijaLLMassistedExtractionRegulatory2025} reported,
in the adjacent problem of GDPR requirement extraction, that
high BERTScore can coexist with low BLEU when meaning is
captured but wording diverges. That pattern characterizes Chat
mode in this study exactly. Chat leads document-level SBERT
cosine similarity and BERTScore Precision, both semantic
measures, while sitting in the middle on ROUGE-2 against the
expert reference; the meaning is captured even where the
wording diverges from the reference's checkbox-style prose.

\sloppy
\textbf{Structured legal-document generation.} The hybrid
recommendation in Section~\ref{sec:disc-implications}, visible
scaffolding paired with LLM extraction, is architecturally
consonant with the wrapper-and-iterate design Nigam et al.\
\cite{nigamStructuredLegalDocument2025} report for Indian
private legal-document drafting. Their argument that
orchestration over scale matters, that the structural wrapper
around a smaller model can match a frontier model, finds an
empirical counterpart on the user-experience side here. Holding
the model constant and varying only the interaction surface
produces sharp preference and quality differences across the
three modes; the orchestration layer demonstrably carries the
load that the brief flagged as an open question. The other two
structured-legal works surveyed in Background sit further from
the design choices of this study and do not warrant a return
visit at this point.
\fussy

\textbf{Evaluation methodology.} Su et al.\
\cite{suJuDGEBenchmarkingJudgment2025} argued, on the basis of
Chinese judgment-document generation, that lexical metrics alone
cannot capture the legal reasoning that distinguishes a sound
judgment from a plausible-sounding one, and proposed pairing
those metrics with judgment-specific dimensions. The data here
partially complicates that argument and partially supports it.
The complication is that the lexical metrics (BLEU, ROUGE,
METEOR) and the BERT-based metrics (BERTScore, SBERT) order the
three modes the same way; on the present sample they appear to
capture related signals rather than orthogonal ones. The
support is that the judge layer adds GDPR-specific dimensions
(Completeness, Scenario Faithfulness, Hallucination) that no
reference-based metric in this study could surface, and that
those dimensions reveal the central confidence--correctness gap
of this thesis. The LLM-as-a-judge is therefore a
complementary, not a replacement, signal: it does work the
reference-based metrics cannot do, and it does so in a way
consistent with the dual-evaluation template prior work has
converged on.

\textbf{User-facing legal AI.} Surya et al.\
\cite{suryaAIPoweredInteractiveLegal2025} and Vayadande et al.\
\cite{vayadandeAIPoweredLegalDocumentation2024} validated
user-facing legal AI through illustrative use cases without
controlled novice studies; both works closed by acknowledging
the absence of empirical user evaluation as a limitation. This
thesis fills that exact gap. The confidence--correctness
decoupling reported in Section~\ref{sec:disc-gap}, with novices
preferring the most fabricated mode and feeling most confident
about the least complete one, is the kind of empirical finding
inspection-based evaluations cannot produce. It is also the
finding most directly relevant to the deployment of
user-facing legal AI for non-experts, the constituency both
works targeted.

\sloppy
The Background closed by naming the unfilled intersection of
three concerns: GDPR-specific structured output, novice users
without legal training, and explicit measurement of the
confidence--quality gap under varying levels of LLM support.
This study is the first to occupy that intersection. The
substantive contribution is the gap itself, the architectural
explanation for why Ask underperforms despite its conceptual
appeal, and the design hierarchy that follows from both.
\fussy

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limits}

The study's design choices bound the findings in six concrete
ways.

\textbf{Single scenario.} Every participant produced ROPAs for
the same fictional scenario (APOR~GmbH, employee and parking
management). The scenario covers the GDPR Article~30 fields and
is accessible to novices, but the findings are tied to its
specific shape. A scenario that asked for more retention
specifics, less checkbox-mappable content, or fewer numbered
legal references might shift the chapter-level distribution of
leadership, even if the direction of the trade-off survives.

\textbf{Novice-only sample.} Participants were university
students, self-reported as ROPA-novice, with a mean ROPA
familiarity of $1.45$ on the $1$--$5$ scale. The findings are
explicitly novice-applicable. Whether the design hierarchy
generalizes to experts is an open question. A GDPR-trained data
protection officer presented with the same three modes might
prefer Ask, because the conceptual-explainer loop is a faster
path to a document when the user already knows the legal
vocabulary.

\textbf{IT-leaning, chatbot-fluent demographic.} The novice
sample is not a generic novice sample. Of those who reported
gender, $62.1\,\%$ identified as male; the highest completed
degree was almost uniformly Bachelor-level, with field of study
or work concentrated in IT and Computer Science; chatbot
familiarity sat at a mean of $4.36$ on the $1$--$5$ scale
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-demographics}, Figures~\ref{fig:demographics-gender}--\ref{fig:demographics-field}).
This profile is more chatbot-fluent than the broader novice
population GDPR compliance drafters have in mind, the small or
medium-sized organization staff member with no IT background
and no prior chatbot exposure. The likely direction of that
confound is conservative for the present findings rather than
inflationary: a less tech-comfortable population, less
practiced at interacting with a freeform chatbot, would
plausibly show a \emph{wider} Form-over-Chat preference gap and
a sharper Ask-as-explainer-fails-novices reading, not a
narrower one. The Form-preference finding and the
architectural argument about Ask are therefore conservative
against the broader audience the tool is built for.

\textbf{Single reference document.} The NLP metrics score
against a single expert-authored Form-style reference. Form
documents may score higher on lexical metrics partly because
they share the reference's surface form rather than because
they share its content. Two pieces of evidence suggest the
framing bias does not exhaust the Form Recall lead: the
BERT-based metrics show the same direction, and the judge
(which does not use the reference at all) reports the same
Completeness pattern.

\textbf{Single judge model.} The LLM-as-a-judge layer is a
single-pass, single-model assessment without inter-rater
replication or legal-expert validation. It is reported as a
supplementary signal throughout the Evaluation chapter, and its
scores are not subjected to inferential testing. The judge's
substantive claims align with the NLP metrics and with the case
study, which gives the judge layer a triangulation grounding it
would not have on its own, but the judge is not a substitute
for expert review.

\textbf{German-primary corpus, English study materials.} The
ROPA documents are predominantly German with English glosses;
the reference document is German; the judge prompts are in
English. The metric absolute values, particularly the lexical
ones, are affected by this mixture. The direction of the
per-mode trade-off survives across the metric families, which
suggests the language mix is not the primary driver, but
cross-language calibration of the NLP pipeline is a follow-up
this study does not perform.


\section{Summary}
\label{sec:disc-summary}

The chapter set out to make sense of a hypothesis reversal. The
prior expectation that more LLM involvement would translate
into more user preference did not survive contact with the
data; novices preferred Form, the mode that does the least LLM
work for them. The explanation runs through RQ3. Confidence and
correctness are decoupled in this novice population: the mode
users feel most confident about produces the least complete
documents, and the mode users prefer to re-use produces the
most fabricated ones. The two-participant case study makes the
gap concrete at human scale, and the judge's holistic Overall
scores, all below the rubric midpoint, locate the comparison
among three routes to a moderate document rather than between
moderate and good documents. What links the three streams is
how each mode populates the document object the shared
final-generation step receives. Form lets the user populate
the scaffolding directly; Chat lets the LLM populate it from
the conversation; Ask leaves the scaffolding untouched and the
user writes only the per-section free-text fields. Ask
underperforms because the novice user cannot bear sole
authorship of a GDPR document; Form's Completeness--Hallucination
co-occurrence is an artifact of the shared generation step
being handed the most to render. Read against the four lenses
of the prior literature, the substantive contribution is the
confidence--quality gap itself, an empirical finding the
inspection-based prior work on user-facing legal AI was not
positioned to produce. The thesis-level claim that follows is
that LLM-assisted document tooling for novices should position
the LLM as a co-author rather than as a teacher, and that
visible structure with LLM-driven population is the most
promising hybrid the data supports.

The Conclusion chapter (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}) returns to
RQ1, RQ2, and RQ3 in plain English and draws the design lessons
this Discussion has framed.
